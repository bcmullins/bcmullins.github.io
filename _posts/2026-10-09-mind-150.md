[Mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mind_(journal)) is a premier journal of philosophy, publishing articles on epistemology, metaphysics, and more, and has been aligned with the [analytic tradition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_philosophy) throughout most of its history. Mind was founded in 1876 by the philosopher and psychologist [Alexander Bain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Bain_(philosopher)) as the first British academic journal devoted to philosophy and matters of the mind (the [Journal of Speculative Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Speculative_Philosophy) from the [St. Louis Hegelians]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2026-01-02-interesting-articles-2025.md %}#philosophy-of-the-people-how-prairie-philosophy-democratized-thought-in-19th-century-america) first appeared in 1867). From the start, Mind was jam-packed with articles from eminent philosophers, psychologists, and other Victorian intellectuals. It published some heavy hitters from the twentieth century, including Bertrand Russell's [On Denoting](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2248381) (1905), which set the stage for the [logical analysis](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logical-atomism/) of language, and Alan Turing's [Computing Machinery and Intelligence](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2251299) (1950), which introduced the [Turing Test](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/turing-test/). Over the past fifty years, articles from Mind have regularly appeared in the [Philosopher's Annual](https://philosophersannual.org/), a yearly compilation of the "best" new articles in philosophy.

Mind emerged during a pivot from the generalist intellectual culture of the mid-Victorian era to the rising professionalization of modern academic disciplines. Prior to its founding, major British philosophical and early psychological debates were largely waged in the pages of high-brow, wide-audience periodicals like the [Fortnightly Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Fortnightly_Review) and the [Contemporary Review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Contemporary_Review), which catered to an educated public rather than a specialized scholarly silo. At the same time, the 1870s saw the rapid splintering of [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) into technical publications like [Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_(journal)) (1869) and [Brain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brain_(journal)) (1878), which formalized distinct scientific fields around methodological traditions and institutional focus. Mind bridged this gap by carving out the first dedicated, specialized venue strictly for mental philosophy, demanding the technical precision of the newly emerging scientific journals while offering a forum for debates over consciousness, epistemology, and ethics that had previously filled the generalist magazines.

# First Issue

The first issue of Mind is a mixed bag. We begin with some prefatory words from the editor, [George Croom Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Croom_Robertson), which delineates the boundaries of the journal around the study of the mind with the goal of forging a science of the mental. This issue contains a mix of psychology and philosophy articles. The former are more physiological and feel close to what one might read in Nature. The latter would largely be at home in the Fortnightly Review. Despite its lack of voice, the first issue of Mind is an interesting snapshot of the state of philosophy and psychology in the 1870s from an impressive roster of contributors. Below, I survey each of the articles in this inaugural issue, which you can read in full [via the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/mindreview01edinuoft/page/n5/mode/2up).

## Physiological Psychology in Germany

#### [James Sully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Sully)

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/wundt.jpg">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">A portrait of Wilhelm Wundt</figcaption>
</figure>

The study of the mind in mid-nineteenth century Germany was largely dominated by the metaphysical approach, e.g., following Kant and Herbart. [Wilhelm Wundt's](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wilhelm-wundt/) work in the latter half of the century brought the methods of laboratory science to bear on psychology. This article surveys and provides context for Wundt's [Principles of Physiological Psychology](https://archive.org/details/principlesofphys00wundiala/page/n5/mode/2up) (1874), which introduced experimental ideas to a wide audience of researchers. 

Wundt's approach is built around a posited correspondence between mental and physical events such as visual perception and stimulation of the nervous system. By isolating the latter in a laboratory setting, e.g., through measuring reaction times, we can learn how the mind structures these basic sensations and processes. Moreover, by focusing exclusively on measurable phenomena, Wundt sidesteps metaphysical issues lurking in the external world. 

## Consistency and Real Inference

#### [John Venn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Venn)

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img width="225" height="300" src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/venn.jpg">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">A caricature of John Venn</figcaption>
</figure>

The scope of [syllogistic logic]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2026-07-01-research-from-1826.md %}#elements-of-logic) sparked debate among nineteenth century philosophers. The conceptualist view holds that logic is the analysis of mental concepts, where the laws of logic are the [laws of thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Laws_of_Thought). On this view, propositions make reference only to general notions generated internally by consciousness rather than physical things in the world. Versions of conceptualism were advanced by [William Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_William_Hamilton,_9th_Baronet), [Henry Longueville Mansel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Longueville_Mansel), and [George Boole](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/boole/). While conceptualism sounds similar to the abstract and formal way we treat logic today, we typically think that logical truths are not dependent upon human cognitive capacities. 

In contrast, the empiricist or materialist view holds that propositions ultimately make reference to facts about the world. Consider the [Socratic syllogism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogism). The minor premise "Socrates is a man" makes reference to a specific fact.  The major premise "All men are mortal" is obtained by induction from a collection of specific facts. The conclusion "Socrates is mortal" is an implication of a belief we judge to be true rather than a truth about our concepts of "Socrates", "man", and "mortal". Venn leans toward the latter view, especially since the former struggles to make sense of probability.

## Philosophy at Oxford

#### [Mark Pattison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Pattison_(academic))

Each issue in the first volume of Mind contains an article surveying the state of philosophy at a major British university. First up is a bit of an odd choice with [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), since Pattison argues that the light of philosophy is all but extinguished at this [venerable institution](https://www.philosophy.ox.ac.uk/history-of-oxford-philosophy).

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img width="175" height="300" src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/newman.jpg">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">A caricature of John Newman <br/> from Vanity Fair magazine</figcaption>
</figure>

Throughout the nineteenth century, philosophy at Oxford can be characterized by two themes: theological controversy and [Lit Hum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literae_humaniores) examinations. Historically, philosophy and religion have been intimately tied together at Oxford. In the 1830s, an Anglican priest and Fellow of Oriel College, [John Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Newman), argued that the church ought to reestablish its autonomy from the state and return to its ceremonial practices common prior to the reformation. In an interesting twist, Newman would [join the Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Newman#Conversion_to_Catholicism) in 1845, sapping the philosophical spirit of Oxford in the process.

<figure style="float: left; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 15px 0px 0px">
    <img width="200" height="300" src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/pattison.jpg">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">A caricature of Mark Pattison</figcaption>
</figure>

Oxford's Lit Hum course of study spanned Greek and Latin, ancient history, and philosophy and culminated in highly competitive examinations. This approach lent itself to [repetition and narrow study of established works]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2023-11-14-research-from-1873.md %}#liberal-education-in-the-nineteenth-century) rather than advancing new ideas. Moreover, the best and brightest of pupils and tutors were burning out in service of these examinations, even though the results were quite impressive. Though Pattison does not mention it, one cannot help compare this setting to the [research-based Humboldt model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldtian_model_of_higher_education) thriving at the time.

All of this negativity is negated a bit by surveying some recent work by Oxford dons. [Benjamin Jowett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Jowett) translated the dialogues of Plato into English and provided extensive commentary. [T. H. Green](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/green/) published a lengthy commentary prefacing a printing of Hume's [Treatise of Human Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_of_Human_Nature) that became foundational to the coming wave of [British Idealism]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-07-07-research-from-1924.md %}#contemporary-british-philosophy-personal-statements-first-series). [William Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wallace_(philosopher)) published a translation and introduction to Hegel's [Encyclopaedia of the Philosophical Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopaedia_of_the_Philosophical_Sciences_in_Basic_Outline). 

## The Theory of Evolution in its Application to Practice

#### [Henry Sidgwick](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sidgwick/)

The nineteenth century saw a shift in the conception of nature from a fixed entity to that of an evolving process. This change in outlook challenged maxims, such as those of moral philosophy, which some took to hold across time and space. Joseph Butler and the [Scottish Common Sense school]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2025-09-07-research-from-1875.md %}#the-scottish-philosophy), for instance, posit a mental faculty to intuit the rightness or wrongness of actions. Sidgwick argues that this is undermined by biological evolution since there is some time at which humans or human ancestors developed such intuition, and there may be human descendants that possess other sorts of intuitions or moral sense. This is in addition to numerous other difficulties this view faces such as temporal and cross-cultural inconsistencies.

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img width="250" height="300" src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/sidgwick.jpg">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">A portrait of Henry Sidgwick</figcaption>
</figure>

Sidgwick has a [peculiar view of moral philosophy]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-11-22-research-from-1874.md %}#the-methods-of-ethics) as more prescriptive rather than normative, i.e., explaining right action rather than determining whether or not an action is right. Can evolutionary theory determine such a moral philosophy? [Herbert Spencer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spencer/) has argued that [societies tend to develop]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2023-11-14-research-from-1873.md %}#the-study-of-sociology) toward states of greater happiness, which points to a version of [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism). Sidgwick responds that maximizing happiness frequently runs counter to evolutionary pressures, though he focuses more on survival rather than reproduction. 

A more coherent intuitionist view is that we reason about moral matters through [sympathy and other such notions](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-sentimentalism/).  These psychological features evolved gradually whether socially or biologically. While evolutionary theory can inform how these sympathies developed and lead to the survival of our ancestors, Sidgwick argues that it does not provide a rational justification for why we ought to follow these intuitions. 


## Philosophy and Science

#### [Shadworth Hodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadworth_Hodgson)

The 1870s saw [no shortage]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-11-22-research-from-1874.md %}#the-great-conflict) of analyses [mapping the terrains]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-11-22-research-from-1874.md %}#address-delivered-before-the-british-association-assembled-at-belfast) of science and religion, especially where they were at loggerheads. Hodgson's project, which spans the first three issues in the first volume of Mind, is to delineate science from philosophy while retaining a substantive role for the latter. One view, which Hodgson calls English Positivism, holds that philosophy consists of preliminary groundwork and its problems are those that have not yet become sciences. A less diminutive view, [Comtean Positivism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/comte/#ClaSciSci), sees the role of philosophy as wrangling scientific theories.

>It is possible so to draw the line between them that nothing remains for philosophy but the preliminary guesses at truth which men have made before striking in at the true methods of discovery, which true methods with their results are science, and supersede the old mistakes which are philosophy.

Hodgson advances a far more expansive role for philosophy. The sciences study objective phenomena: the mass of an object, the market price of a good, the structure of a chemical bond. What's left out of this view of science is subjectivity or how one experiences these phenomena, which forms the domain of philosophy. Taking an expansive view of existence,  Hodgson sneaks in much of traditional metaphysics as the interaction between the objective and the subjective. 

## The Comparative Psychology of Man

#### [Herbert Spencer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spencer/)

In the latter half of the nineteenth century, Herbert Spencer engaged in a [wide-ranging project]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2023-11-14-research-from-1873.md %}#the-study-of-sociology) to show that evolution (or [his understanding of it](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer#Evolution)) is a law of nature governing the mind and society, the material and the metaphysical.  This article is a call-to-action noticing that the comparative study of people across cultures is a fruitful avenue to better understanding the mental and the social. 

Roughly, Spencer views evolution as a progressive change from the homogeneous to the heterogeneous, sharing aspects with [Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_three_stages), [Lamarck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism), and [Darwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection). Not only do minds evolve through cognitive ability and capacity for expression, but there's a complex interplay between individuals and society. The structure of individual minds puts limits on the structure of society, and changes to the social precipitate changes in the individual. Obviously, a lot of Spencer's ideas lack empirical support and are [justly open to criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Spencer#Social_Darwinism_and_racial_views).

## The Early Life of James Mill

#### [Alexander Bain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Bain_(philosopher))

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img width="250" height="250" src="/images/blog/look_back/1824/jamesMill.png">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">An ink portrait of Mill <br/> from the Online Library of Liberty</figcaption>
</figure>

[James Mill](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/james-mill/) was an [influential philosopher and political economist]({{ site.baseurl }}{% link _posts/2024-12-29-research-from-1824.md %}#essays-for-the-supplement-to-the-encyclopedia-britannica) in early nineteenth-century Britain. He was born in rural Scotland and recognized for his mental prowess early on. Mill attended [Montrose Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montrose_Academy) for secondary school until entering the [University of Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Edinburgh#18th_and_19th_century) in the 1780s. Though taking orders with the Church of Scotland following his studies, Mill was not cut out to be a preacher, being perceived as obscure. After shutting that door, he spent over a decade as a tutor, continuing his studies on philosophy and political economy while making friends in high places. 

This sketch is part of Bain's work on a [biography of James Mill](https://archive.org/details/jamesmillbiograp00bainiala/page/n7/mode/2up), later published in 1882. The most interesting part is seeing what Mill was reading from the library during his Divinity studies in Edinburgh. These range from recent classics such as Adam Ferguson's [Essay on the History of Civil Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_on_the_History_of_Civil_Society) (1767), [Rousseau's Emile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile,_or_On_Education) (1762), and Adam Smith's [The Theory of Moral Sentiments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Theory_of_Moral_Sentiments) (1759) to old favorites such as [Plato's dialogues](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/plato-the-dialogues-of-plato-in-5-vols-jowett-ed). 

## Notes from the Editors

Robertson discusses an [experiment described by Aristotle](https://www.newscientist.com/article/1932228-tactile-illusions-1-the-aristotle-illusion/) where one contorts their fingers, causing an unusual sensation that's both closer and farther away from the finger tip, indicating a spatial component to perception by touch. Despite my best efforts, I could not reproduce the hand manipulation.

<figure style="float: right; display: inline-block; margin: 0px 0px 0px 15px">
    <img width="250" height="250" src="/images/blog/look_back/mind/hume.png">
    <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 12px;">An ink portrait of David Hume <br/> from the Online Library of Liberty</figcaption>
</figure>

Bain makes clear two views on the [uniformity of nature](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/induction-problem/#HumeProb). Following Hume's conception, Bain holds that the uniformity of nature is an assumption necessary to inductive inference. In his [Problems of Life and Mind](https://archive.org/details/problemsoflifemi01leweiala), in contrast, [George Henry Lewes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Henry_Lewes) argues that the uniformity of nature is a tautology. 

[The Syllabus of Plane Geometry](https://archive.org/details/syllabusofplaneg00willrichz) is a text published by the [Association for the Improvement of Geometrical Teaching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Association#History) in 1875 as a guide to studying [Euclid's Elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid%27s_Elements). Robertson takes issue with the guide's preface on logic, which details the contrapositive, converse, and inverse for a [propositional statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_logic). In particular, Robertson takes issue with the idea that a proposition and its contrapositive are equivalent, which seems not to hold on his view of [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_logic) due to technicalities with quantification.
